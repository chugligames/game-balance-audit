# Benchmarks, with sources

Numbers to compare simulation results with. Every row was checked against its source in
October 2026; the quality column says how much weight it can carry (**paper**, **official**:
the publisher or developer, **industry blog**, **community data**, **anecdote**;
"secondary" means the page reports someone else's data). Where nothing public exists, the
table says so. Don't fill those gaps with a guess.

Quote a benchmark with its source, and say when it comes from a different kind of game or
player population. Bots aren't people: compare *shapes and orderings* first, and absolute
numbers only after calibration.

**How to use.** Turn the cap and regeneration into the free energy each player profile gets
per day: a player who returns every few hours refills to the cap, so their daily energy is

**Caveats.** Wiki numbers change with live updates. Session and retention benchmarks mix
every genre.

**How to use.** Model "engagement" (the share of DAU who watch at least one RV) as roughly 25–45%. Treat placement context as the lever: offers at a resource wall convert better than offers between levels (38% against 24%).

India rewarded eCPM is roughly 10× below the US, but the only India figure has an unverified origin. Sweep $0.5–3 for India against $12–30 for the US, and remember Q4 runs seasonally high.
The 4.5× IAP and 50% retention figures are selection effects, because engaged players both watch and pay. Do not simulate them as a causal effect of adding ads.

**How to use.** Keep daily and monthly conversion apart. About 0.2–1% of DAU pay on a given day in median casual or puzzle games. Monthly payers run about 2–5% of MAU. Top IAP-led portfolios reach about 4.5% daily.

The 2018 GameAnalytics figures are old and were read off charts, so use them as order-of-magnitude anchors only.

**How to use.** The best causal evidence is about difficulty walls, not energy timers. Two randomised experiments (EA, and Ascarza et al.) found that easing hard spots raised engagement 4–20% without lowering revenue, and in one case raised it. Model churn as a hazard that rises with consecutive failures and level difficulty.
The cost of a wall shows up as churn among future payers, so a stricter energy wall needs a churn term that is higher than its extra ad views.
Interstitials carry several times the per-impression churn of rewarded ads.
The preference surveys come from ad sellers (Tapjoy, Facebook, AdMob). Use them to argue for rewarded over forced ads, not to set magnitudes.

### Biggest gaps

- **Rewarded views per daily player, by genre**, and the split between energy or lives
  refills and other placements. No public data.
- **The effect of energy caps or refill-timer length on retention.** No A/B result is
  published. The closest evidence is the two difficulty-wall experiments above.
- **Merge-genre D7/D30 retention and payer conversion.** None public.

**How to use.** Simulate attempts per completion, not just a single pass/fail result. A level-1-to-100 curve should average about 2–4 attempts per completion after onboarding, with spikes of about 5–7 average and long tails of 20–30+. Put a hard level about every 4–5 levels and a bigger spike about every 10. Flag any level whose simulated attempts jump sharply between levels 20 and 80, because that is where the EA and Ascarza data show churn tracking difficulty most closely.

**Caveats.** Published pass rates come from survivors, since weaker players churn first and later levels look easier than they are. Within-level RNG (seed spread 0.15–0.75) can outweigh level design, so run many seeds per level. Bot pass rates need calibrating to human data. Roohi et al. gave their RL agent 4× the human move budget and found that AI pass rate alone was a poor predictor of human pass rate.

**How to use.** Report seat win rates with confidence intervals. Two-player edges of about 52–55% are normal in respected games (chess, Carcassonne, Dominion). The Go authority moved komi at about 52%, and players start asking for a rebalance near 55%. With N players, compare each seat against 1/N; the Feast for Odin 3p first seat at 36% against 33.3% is about 1.08×. For skill and luck, run bots of graded strength and compute p_sd or the win rate of strong against weak bots. Compare that with Duersch's 56.4% "half-chance" line.

**Caveats.** Strong bots inflate seat advantage (7WD: 66.8% for bots against 55.7% for humans), so calibrate the bot's strength before you report a seat edge. Luck metrics depend on the player population; for example, a BGA casual pool makes Race for the Galaxy look like pure luck. Measure lead change and the midgame-leader win rate in your own simulations; no external benchmark for them exists.

**How to use.** Don't rank levels by state-space size or by raw search nodes or backtracks; in these studies they barely predict human solve time. Use the minimal solution length as a baseline; it works well for Rush Hour and poorly for Sokoban. Best of all is a human-like solver that wanders, avoids dead states and restarts, run many times. Average its steps.

**Caveats.** These correlations come from small, uniform level sets (for example, all 4-box Sokoban). Validate against a few human playtests before trusting the ranking, and expect solver metrics to predict experts better than novices.

**How to use.** Treat "target win rate" as something set per level type. For example, normal levels might sit around 50–85% per attempt and hard spikes around 15–35% (about 3–7 attempts), consistent with Lily's Garden and Ascarza. Spikes should be rare, signposted, and followed by easier levels. Easier generally keeps casual players longer (Lomas, Ascarza, Roohi's data), so a skill should flag sustained runs of hard levels, not single spikes.

**Caveats.** The 85% figure is a learning-theory optimum, not a measured player preference. Every live-game number here is filtered by survivorship and by boosters or monetized retries.
